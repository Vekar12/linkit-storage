Bank-Side Digital Signature on CFAF

Section 01

Problem Definition

The current CFAF execution workflow requires both parties — the bank representative (sales user) and the customer — to complete Aadhaar-based OTP e-signatures via SignDesk. While Aadhaar OTP is appropriate for customer authentication (identity-linked consent), it introduces unnecessary friction, latency, and dependency on the bank side where the signatory is a known, authenticated, internal employee operating within the bank's own systems.

Objective

Replace the bank-side Aadhaar OTP e-signature with a digital signature mechanism that is:

  • Legally equivalent or superior to Aadhaar eSign under Indian IT Act 2000
  • Zero additional vendor cost — handled entirely within CACM/bank infrastructure
  • Automated and scalable — no per-transaction manual intervention
  • Compatible with TCS platform — minimal integration effort
  • Signing order preserved — bank representative signs first, then customer eSign via SignDesk
Section 02

Shortlisted Options

After evaluating all possible methods against the constraints (no new vendor, no additional cost, legal compliance, scalability), two viable options emerge. A third is included as the minimum-viable baseline only.

A
Server-Side DSC via Existing HSM
Recommended

A Class 3 Digital Signature Certificate (DSC) is issued to the bank's authorized signatory (or as an organizational certificate) and stored on the bank's existing Hardware Security Module (HSM) — the same infrastructure that secures UPI, RTGS, NEFT, and net banking cryptographic operations. When the CFAF is generated on the TCS platform, the system makes an API call to the HSM, which applies the DSC to the PDF. No human touches the signing process.

Legal Basis
IT Act 2000, Section 3 — Digital Signature via asymmetric cryptography. Section 5 — legally equivalent to wet ink signature. Section 15 — qualifies as "Secure Digital Signature".
Incremental Cost
₹0 per transaction. HSM infrastructure already exists. DSC renewal is ₹1,500–3,000/year for Class 3 organizational certificate.
Scalability
Unlimited. HSMs are designed for millions of cryptographic operations/day. No per-transaction bottleneck.
Implementation Effort
Medium. Requires TCS platform to invoke HSM signing API during CFAF PDF generation. Estimated 2–4 weeks with TCS coordination.
Legal Precedent & Regulatory Standing
IT Act Section 3: A digital signature created using asymmetric crypto with a licensed CA certificate is the highest form of electronic authentication recognized under Indian law. It is the only method that produces a "secure digital signature" (Section 15) — meaning the burden of proof shifts to the challenger, not the bank.

Indian Evidence Act Section 85B: Presumes that a secure electronic record has not been altered — the court assumes authenticity unless proven otherwise. This is a critical advantage over all other methods.

CERSAI Mandate: Banks already use DSCs (via HSM) for charge registration filings. This is extending an existing, proven workflow to one more document type.

RBI Digital Lending Guidelines (Aug 2022): Mandate that executed loan agreements be delivered digitally at disbursement. A DSC-signed PDF is the cleanest form of compliance.
Why This Wins

Strongest legal standing + Zero marginal cost + Already-deployed infrastructure

The private key never leaves the HSM hardware, making it tamper-proof and audit-ready. Every signature carries a timestamp, certificate chain, and cryptographic proof — verifiable by any standard PDF reader (Adobe Acrobat, Foxit). In any legal dispute, the document is self-proving under Section 85B of the Evidence Act. No other option provides this level of legal immunity.

B
Software-Based DSC (.pfx Certificate on Server)
Viable Alternative

A Class 2 or Class 3 DSC is issued to the authorized signatory, exported as a .pfx / PKCS#12 file, and stored on the TCS application server. The TCS platform loads the certificate and applies it during CFAF PDF generation — no HSM required.

Legal Basis
Identical to Option A — IT Act Section 3, Section 5, Section 15. Legally, a .pfx-signed document is indistinguishable from HSM-signed.
Incremental Cost
₹500–1,500/year per certificate. Negligible.
Scalability
High. Limited only by server compute — handles thousands of signings per minute easily.
Implementation Effort
Low. Standard PDF signing libraries (iText, Apache PDFBox) support .pfx natively. TCS platform likely already has this capability. Estimated 1–2 weeks.
Key Difference from Option A

The .pfx file is a software artifact — it can theoretically be copied if the server is compromised. While legally equivalent, it requires strict access controls (file-system permissions, key password, audit logging). HSM provides hardware-level protection that a .pfx cannot match. For an organization of ICICI's size and regulatory scrutiny, HSM is the superior posture.

C
Embedded Scanned Signature Image
Baseline Only

A high-resolution scan of the authorized signatory's wet signature is embedded directly into the CFAF PDF template. The document is generated with the image pre-placed — no cryptographic operation occurs.

Legal Basis
NOT a "digital signature" under IT Act. Admissible under Evidence Act Section 65B only with a supporting certificate. Contract validity relies on Section 10A (electronic contracts are valid) and estoppel.
Incremental Cost
₹0. Pure template change.
Scalability
Unlimited. It's just an image in a template.
Implementation Effort
Trivial. Embed image in CFAF template. Can be done in a day.
Legal Risk

A scanned image carries no cryptographic proof. It cannot prove when the document was signed, whether the document was altered after signing, or who authorized the signature. Under Section 85B of the Evidence Act, this does NOT qualify for the presumption of authenticity — the bank must independently prove the document's integrity in any dispute. While widely used across banking for standard-form agreements today, this is a legacy practice that is progressively being replaced.

Section 05

Recommended End-to-End Signing Flow

The bank representative signs first. The rationale: the CFAF is the bank's offer — the bank's authorized signatory executes the agreement terms, and then the customer accepts by adding their Aadhaar eSign. This order ensures the customer always receives and signs a document that already bears the bank's commitment.

CFAF Execution Flow — Bank DSC First, Then Customer Aadhaar eSign
1
TCS Platform — Document Generation
CFAF PDF is generated with all customer & product details populated
Triggered by: Sales user initiates agreement after product selection & credit approval
2
HSM — Automated Bank-Side DSC Signing
TCS platform calls internal HSM API → DSC applied to bank representative signature field
No manual intervention. Private key never leaves HSM. Signature includes timestamp + certificate chain. Takes <200ms.
3
TCS Platform — Handoff to SignDesk
Bank-signed CFAF PDF is transmitted to SignDesk via existing API integration
The document now carries the bank's cryptographic signature and is ready for customer execution
4
SignDesk — Customer Aadhaar OTP eSign
Customer receives CFAF → reviews → authenticates via Aadhaar OTP → eSign applied
Existing flow — no change to customer experience or SignDesk integration
5
Final — Dual-Signed CFAF Archived
Fully executed CFAF (Bank DSC + Customer Aadhaar eSign) returned to TCS platform → archived in DMS
Both signatures independently verifiable. Document is tamper-evident. Ready for CERSAI filing, audit, or dispute resolution.

Why Bank Signs First (Not After Customer)

  • Legal clarity: CFAF is the bank's offer of facility — the bank's signature represents commitment to terms. Customer's eSign is acceptance. Offer → Acceptance is the correct contractual sequence under Indian Contract Act, 1872.
  • Operational efficiency: Bank-side DSC is automated (sub-second). Placing it first means no dependency on customer action — the document is pre-signed and waiting. If customer delays, the bank's signature is already done.
  • Tamper assurance: Customer receives a document with cryptographic proof that the bank committed to those exact terms. Any alteration after bank signing would break the DSC — the customer knows they're signing an authentic, unaltered bank document.
  • SignDesk compatibility: SignDesk receives a pre-signed PDF and adds the customer's Aadhaar eSign as a second signature layer. This is standard multi-party signing — no SignDesk workflow changes required.
Section 06

Legal Framework & Statutory References

IT Act 2000 — Section 3: Authentication of Electronic Records
Specifies that authentication of an electronic record is accomplished by means of an asymmetric crypto system and hash function which envelop and transform the initial electronic record into another electronic record. A DSC issued by a licensed Certifying Authority (CA) and applied via HSM satisfies this provision completely.
IT Act 2000 — Section 5: Legal Recognition of Electronic Signatures
"Where any law provides that information or any other matter shall be authenticated by affixing the signature... such requirement shall be deemed to have been satisfied if such information or matter is authenticated by means of digital signature." This makes a DSC-signed CFAF legally identical to a wet-ink-signed paper CFAF.
IT Act 2000 — Section 15: Secure Digital Signature
A digital signature is "secure" if: (a) it was unique to the subscriber at the time applied, (b) it was capable of identifying the subscriber, (c) it was created in a manner under the exclusive control of the subscriber, and (d) it was linked to the electronic record in a manner that any alteration would invalidate the signature. HSM-based DSC meets all four criteria.
Indian Evidence Act — Section 85B: Presumption as to Electronic Records and Digital Signatures
"In any proceedings involving a secure electronic record, the Court shall presume... that the secure electronic record has not been altered since the specific point of time to which the secure status relates." This is the most powerful legal protection available — the court assumes authenticity, and the burden shifts entirely to the party challenging the document.
Indian Evidence Act — Section 65B: Admissibility of Electronic Records
All electronic records require a 65B certificate for admissibility. However, when a document carries a "secure digital signature" under Section 15 of IT Act, Section 85B's presumption applies in addition to 65B compliance — providing a double layer of legal protection that scanned signatures cannot match.
RBI Master Direction on Digital Lending — September 2, 2022
Key provisions: (1) A copy of the executed loan agreement must be provided to the borrower at the time of sanction/disbursement. (2) All communications with the borrower must be in electronic form and stored. While the Direction does not mandate DSC specifically, a DSC-signed PDF is the most robust form of compliance — the document is self-authenticating, timestamped, and verifiable without any supporting infrastructure.
Key Judicial Precedents
Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer (2014, Supreme Court): Strict Section 65B certification required for all electronic evidence from institutional parties. Banks must comply — but DSC-signed documents additionally benefit from Section 85B presumption.

Trimex International v. Vedanta Aluminium (2010, Supreme Court): Recognized electronic records and digital signatures under IT Act as valid for contract formation.

SARFAESI Section 13(2) notice cases (various DRTs): Notices bearing only scanned signatures have been challenged. Tribunals have upheld most on estoppel grounds, but the inconsistency underscores the risk. A DSC-signed notice would be unchallengeable on authenticity.
Section 07

Final Recommendation

Recommended Solution

Option A — Server-Side DSC via Existing HSM Infrastructure

Implement automated bank-side digital signature on CFAF using a Class 3 DSC stored on ICICI Bank's existing HSM infrastructure. The TCS platform invokes the HSM signing API during CFAF PDF generation (Step 2 in the flow above), applying the bank representative's digital signature before the document is handed off to SignDesk for customer Aadhaar OTP eSign.

This delivers the highest legal standing (secure digital signature under Section 15, with Section 85B presumption of authenticity), zero per-transaction cost, sub-second execution, and no new vendor or infrastructure. It aligns with peer bank practices (HDFC, SBI, Axis, Yes Bank) and future-proofs against inevitable RBI mandates for DSC on customer agreements.

Fallback

If HSM integration faces delays due to TCS platform constraints, deploy Option B (Software DSC) as an interim measure within 1–2 weeks. The legal standing is identical. Migrate to HSM when integration is ready — no document format or workflow changes needed, only the signing backend changes.
